\chapter{Introduction}
\label{cha:introduction}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Random numbers are used in many areas, including cryptography, digital signatures, data anonymisation, and simulation. They are one of the most basic building blocks for system security and correctness. 

There are two common types of random number sources. The first type is the pseudo-random number generator (PRNG), which uses a deterministic algorithm. It starts from a short initial value, called a \emph{seed}, and expands it into a long sequence of numbers that look random. As long as the seed is kept secret, the output is unpredictable. The catch is that the seed itself must be genuinely hard to guess - what is technically called a \emph{high-entropy} value, meaning it carries enough true unpredictability that an attacker cannot reproduce it. A PRNG therefore does not remove the need for true randomness; it only shifts that need to producing a good seed. 

The second type is the true random number generator (TRNG), which uses physical processes such as electronic noise - for instance, the on-chip RDRAND/RDSEED instructions in modern Intel CPUs~\citep{mathew2012intel} - or quantum sources such as photon detection and vacuum-fluctuation measurement, which are surveyed in detail by~\citet{herrero2017qrng} and have been commercialised as products like ID Quantique's Quantis. TRNGs provide real unpredictability, but they require special hardware, are expensive to deploy, and are not easy to access on ordinary user devices.

In recent years, a middle-ground approach has attracted research interest: can we extract randomness from high-entropy natural data sources that already exist and are publicly accessible? Candidates studied in the literature include blockchain data such as Bitcoin block hashes~\citep{bonneau2015bitcoin,pierrot2018malleability} and financial market data; the latter is the focus of this thesis. It is produced by a large number of participants at very high frequency, contains microstructure noise that is hard to model, and shows complexity that is hard to compress beyond simple statistical structure. Several previous works have explored this idea under the name \emph{financial randomness beacon}~\citep{clark2010beacon,chiba2024lfsr,landis2025beacon}, trying to turn public market data into a trusted random source.

However, using the sign of price changes directly as a random bit stream does not work. Many studies have shown that high-frequency trading sequences look balanced at the marginal level (for example, the up/down ratio is close to 1:1), but neighbouring symbols are strongly correlated at short lags. A common cause is microstructure noise such as the bid-ask bounce~\citep{cont2001stylized,shternshis2025predictability}. To turn this kind of ``looks random but is conditionally predictable'' data into a qualified random bit stream, the key question is: can suitable time aggregation bypass these short-range dependencies so that the resulting sequence passes standard randomness tests? If so, on which ``time'' axis should we aggregate, and how coarse should the aggregation be?

This thesis focuses on the cryptocurrency spot market. Compared to traditional stock markets, the cryptocurrency market runs 24~hours a day without exchange closures, and its public APIs (such as Binance) allow direct download of every trade, with no broker batching or routing layer in between, so market activity is directly visible to end users. From an engineering point of view, this makes it more suitable for building a public random number service. Using this data as input, we study the feasibility of the full pipeline of ``aggregation $\Rightarrow$ encoding $\Rightarrow$ testing $\Rightarrow$ application.''

\section{Brief Literature Review}
\label{sec:brief-lit-review}

The literature directly related to this work falls into two categories.

The first category studies financial data as a randomness source. \citet{clark2010beacon} discussed how public financial data can be used as a public random beacon. \citet{chiba2024lfsr} combined cryptocurrency prices with a Linear Feedback Shift Register (LFSR, a basic building block that produces long pseudo-random bit streams from a short internal state) to design a structured extraction scheme. \citet{landis2025beacon} evaluated the security of financial beacons under an active attacker model.

The second category studies the statistical properties of financial data itself. \citet{cont2001stylized} summarised a set of stylised facts about returns, including heavy tails, volatility clustering, and the slow decay of absolute-returns autocorrelation. \citet{bouchaud2002orderbooks} described the microstructure of price formation from an order-book point of view. \citet{shternshis2025predictability} formalised the bid-ask bounce as a testable diagnostic for negative correlation between neighbouring symbols. Together, these works explain why raw financial data cannot be used as a random source directly.

The most directly relevant works are \citet{shternshis2025predictability} and \citet{onofri2025emergence}. The first paper introduces an entropy-based predictability test $D$ based on the Kullback--Leibler divergence. It also shows empirically that consecutive trade-direction signs display a first-order negative correlation caused by the bid-ask bounce. The second paper extends this framework into an ``all-offset'' aggregation construction. On 8~US stocks and 1~ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund, a basket of assets traded as a single security on an exchange), it observes a same-direction relationship: the higher the trades per second, the larger the aggregation level $\ell$ that is needed. \citet{shternshis2022moscow} provides complementary evidence from another market: the efficiency of the Moscow Stock Exchange varies over time and across industry sectors.

We can summarise two methodological gaps in the existing work:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cross-market transfer of method.} A randomness-testing framework for tick-level financial data - the entropy-based predictability test $D$ with the all-offset construction - has been validated on US stocks~\citep{shternshis2025predictability,onofri2025emergence}. We say the framework ``works'' on a dataset if some aggregation level $\ell^*$ exists at which the encoded bit streams pass the chosen randomness tests at $\alpha = 0.01$. Whether it works on the continuously-trading cryptocurrency market, and what aggregation level and axis are needed there, has not been systematically studied.
\item \textbf{End-to-end engineering pipeline.} Protocol-level work on financial randomness beacons and bit extraction~\citep{clark2010beacon,chiba2024lfsr,landis2025beacon} and statistical-validation work on the randomness of the underlying tick data~\citep{shternshis2025predictability,onofri2025emergence} have not been combined as a single end-to-end pipeline on cryptocurrency data, from aggregation parameter selection through to a password generator. The generator at the end of the chain forces the two sides to meet: the aggregation level $\ell^*$ selected by the randomness tests is also the operational parameter of the application.
\end{enumerate}

This thesis addresses both gaps. Experiment~2 responds to gap~(1), and along the way explores two aggregation axes (transaction-time and 1-second bar), where we also observe that independence depends on the axis. Experiment~3, Experiment~4, and the Password Generator Prototype together respond to gap~(2).

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

Based on the motivation and the literature gaps above, this thesis answers the three research questions:
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1.] How can we design an aggregation algorithm that extracts statistically independent random sequences from cryptocurrency price data?
\item[RQ2.] To what extent can these sequences pass standard randomness tests?
\item[RQ3.] Can this data-driven randomness be effectively applied to secure password generation?
\end{description}

A note on the scope of RQ3: statistical tests are necessary but not sufficient for cryptographic security. The prototype in this thesis treats market-derived bits as entropy input to a cryptographic component, not directly as a password string. The phrase ``effective application'' in RQ3 is therefore limited to ``providing acceptable entropy for a standard password-conditioning pipeline,'' rather than replacing that pipeline.

\chapter{Background}
\label{cha:background}

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature-review}

This section reviews several related papers and follows the two-category split introduced in the Brief Literature Review. The statistical validation line~\citep{cont2001stylized,shternshis2025predictability,onofri2025emergence,shternshis2022moscow} focuses on the randomness structure of financial time series itself. The application / protocol line~\citep{clark2010beacon,chiba2024lfsr,landis2025beacon} focuses on turning that data into cryptographic components.

\subsection*{Stylised facts and microstructure noise}

On the statistical properties of high-frequency financial time series, \citet{cont2001stylized} summarised a set of ``stylised facts'' from the returns viewpoint. These include heavy-tailed distributions, volatility clustering, slow power-law decay of the autocorrelation of absolute returns, and - most relevant to this thesis - negative autocorrelation at very short lags (microstructure scale) caused by the bid-ask bounce. That is, trade prices switch frequently between the best bid and the best ask, which makes the bit sequence encoded by the sign of $\Delta p$ strongly negatively correlated at neighbouring positions. \citet{shternshis2025predictability} formalised this negative autocorrelation as a testable first-order residual diagnostic, and provided empirical evidence on three low-price stocks: SNAP, F, and CCL.

In addition to the bid-ask bounce, another well-documented stylised fact at the microstructure level is the long memory of trade signs. \citet{lillo2004longmemory} showed on the London Stock Exchange that the signs of executed market orders follow a long-memory process, with autocorrelation that decays slowly. They attribute this to order splitting and herding behaviour. The two stylised facts work in opposite directions on the lag-1 autocorrelation of price-change signs: the bid-ask bounce produces negative autocorrelation by alternating between the bid and the ask, while persistent trade-sign autocorrelation produces positive autocorrelation when a sustained order flow walks the book. Which mechanism dominates depends on the activity regime of the asset.

Together, these works show that any approach which directly treats the ``sign of price change'' as i.i.d.\ random bits is almost certain to fail at the raw tick level. This is the fundamental reason why this thesis treats ``aggregation'' as the core of the method.

\subsection*{Financial data as a randomness source}

This line of work can be split into three complementary levels of abstraction.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Protocol level.} \citet{clark2010beacon} used computational finance tools to estimate that each stock in the Dow Jones 30 (a US index of 30 large-cap companies) provides 6--9 bits of entropy per day. Based on this, they designed a 128-bit verifiable beacon protocol - this answers the question ``does financial data really contain randomness, and can it be turned into a cryptographic seed?''.
\item \textbf{Bit extraction level.} \citet{chiba2024lfsr} used cryptocurrency prices to perturb the sampling interval of an LFSR, produced a bit stream, and ran Diehard tests (a classic randomness test battery, the historical precursor of NIST STS) as a sanity check - this answers ``how can we engineer and whiten market signals into usable random bits?''.
\item \textbf{Adversarial security level.} \citet{landis2025beacon} showed under an active attacker model that manipulating an S\&P~100 beacon (a US index of 100 large-cap companies) would require billions of dollars of capital and millions of dollars of slippage cost - this moves the question from ``how much entropy is there'' to ``is the entropy trustworthy under adversarial conditions?''.
\end{enumerate}

These three works each operate at a different level of abstraction, but none of them connects all the way from ``is the underlying data really random'' to a ``production-grade RNG'' - this is exactly the Brief Literature Review gap~(2) made concrete on cryptocurrency data. This thesis sits at the bit extraction level, but differs from \citet{chiba2024lfsr} in that, at the randomness-evaluation stage, it does not depend on extra LFSR post-processing. Instead, it directly evaluates whether the aggregated raw data itself satisfies modern randomness tests.

\subsection*{Entropy-based predictability and the all-offset construction}

\citet{shternshis2025predictability} proposed an entropy-based predictability test $D$ based on the Kullback--Leibler divergence (formal definition in \cref{sec:background-information}). The test treats $k$ as a user-defined hyper-parameter; \citet{shternshis2025predictability} use the fixed setting $k = 2$ in their~\S4.4 to study first-order pair-of-signs predictability (the bid-ask bounce mentioned above). This thesis adopts the same $k = 2$ setup as a first-order diagnostic, complementary to the adaptive-$k$ main statistic, which gives the methodological starting point for separating first-order structure from higher-order structure. We follow this $D$ test and run it at both adaptive $k$ and fixed $k = 2$.

\citet{onofri2025emergence} further upgraded this framework into an ``all-offset'' construction (which Onofri calls ``$\ell$-samplings''): for each aggregation level $\ell$, it constructs $\ell$ separate bit streams from $\ell$ different starting offsets and tests each independently, then uses a ``pass-rate threshold'' as the acceptance rule for $\ell$. This construction replaces ``does a single $p$-value pass the line'' with ``do most offsets pass the line at the same time'', and uses a box plot of $-\log_{10}(p)$ to directly show how the $p$-value distribution shifts as $\ell$ grows. The paper produces a robust empirical observation on 8 US stocks plus 1 ETF: the higher the trades per second of an asset, the larger the minimum required aggregation level $\ell^*$ (the original paper also notes that some assets show non-monotonic behaviour). This thesis directly reuses this all-offset construction and pass-rate threshold framework, but differs from \citet{onofri2025emergence} in three concrete ways: the market is cryptocurrency rather than US equities; the aggregation axis includes physical time (1-second bars) in addition to transaction time; and the qualitative acceptance criterion of that work is operationalised as a quantitative fixed-threshold gate together with a heuristic relaxed gate (\cref{sec:acceptance-gates} and \cref{sec:time-based-pipeline}).

\subsection*{Market efficiency under stress}

\citet{shternshis2022moscow} used Shannon entropy, KL distance, and Monte Carlo simulation to show that the efficiency of the Moscow Stock Exchange in 2012--2021 changed significantly over time and depended on the industry sector. This is independent evidence from another type of market that ``the properties of financial time series are not stationary''. This observation supports our choice to report the selected $\ell^*$ across monthly time windows rather than treating it as a stable value.

\section{Background Information}
\label{sec:background-information}

This section reviews the core concepts used in the later Methods chapter. All randomness tests are based on the same null hypothesis $H_0$: the sequence is i.i.d. Each test gives a $p$-value, and this thesis uses $\alpha = 0.01$ uniformly as the acceptance threshold.

\textbf{Shannon entropy and conditional unpredictability.} For a binary random variable $X \in \{0, 1\}$, the Shannon entropy is defined as $H(X) = -\sum p(x) \log_2 p(x)$, and reaches its maximum value of 1 when $p(0) = p(1) = 0.5$. It is worth emphasising that a marginal entropy close to 1 does not imply that the sequence is unpredictable - a sequence that simply alternates between 0 and 1 also has marginal distribution 0.5/0.5, but its conditional entropy $H(X_t \mid X_{t-1}) \approx 0$. The criterion for ``is it random'' used in this thesis is always based on conditional distributions or multi-symbol statistics, not on $H$ alone. Throughout this thesis we report the deviation $|H(X) - 1|$ as the \emph{Shannon bias}, used as a summary indicator only and not as an acceptance criterion.

\textbf{Predictability $\boldsymbol{D}$.} The entropy-based predictability test proposed by \citet{shternshis2025predictability} is based on the Kullback--Leibler divergence. It measures how much the joint distribution of consecutive symbol blocks of length $k$ in the sequence deviates from the distribution that would be expected under i.i.d. Under $H_0$ it asymptotically follows a $\chi^2$ distribution with $(s^{k-1} - 1)(s - 1)$ degrees of freedom (which equals $2^{k-1} - 1$ in the binary case). The window length $k$ is a user-chosen hyper-parameter.

\textbf{NIST SP800-22 STS.} The NIST Statistical Test Suite (STS) is a randomness test battery published by NIST. It contains 15 sub-tests in 5 categories: Frequency, Pattern, Entropy / Complexity, Spectral, and Random Walks. Common representative sub-tests include:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Monobit (frequency category).} It uses the standard-normal approximation of $|S_n|/\sqrt{n}$ to detect deviation of the frequency of 1s from 0.5.
\item \textbf{Runs (pattern category).} It splits the sequence into maximal same-symbol segments and uses ``the deviation of the number of segments from the i.i.d.\ expectation'' as the statistic, which is sensitive to correlation between neighbouring bits.
\item \textbf{Approximate Entropy (entropy / complexity category).} It measures the complexity of the sequence by taking the difference of the appearance frequencies of windows of length $m$ and $m + 1$; the window length $m$ is a user-chosen hyper-parameter.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{TestU01 batteries.} It is a randomness test library (used by \citet{onofri2025emergence} on financial data). Per the TestU01 user guide, it contains sub-batteries such as Alphabit (9 sub-tests, friendly to small samples) and Rabbit (26 sub-tests, comprehensive). It complements NIST STS.

\textbf{Aggregated trades and 1-second bars.} Binance provides two main data views: tick-by-tick trades (\texttt{trades}) and aggregated trades (\texttt{aggTrades}). The latter merges trades that happen at the same price, in the same direction, and at the same time into a single record. This reduces data size while keeping the price-change and sign information. Based on these two views, aggregation can be done on the transaction-time axis (by trade count) and on the physical-time axis (by second) respectively.

\textbf{Multiple testing.} The Bonferroni and Šidák corrections control the family-wise Type-I error rate, by tightening the per-test significance level $\alpha$ to $\alpha/N$ or $1 - (1 - \alpha)^{1/N}$. They address the question ``how often does \emph{at least one} of $N$ tests falsely reject $H_0$?''. There is no symmetric correction for the opposite question, ``how often does \emph{at least one} of $N$ tests falsely pass?'', because tightening $\alpha$ makes each individual test more likely to pass and therefore pushes the family-wise pass probability in the wrong direction.

\chapter{Methods}
\label{cha:methods}

This chapter describes all the methods used in this thesis to turn a price sequence into a random bit stream that can be tested. It is organised in the order ``encoding $\to$ construction $\to$ tests $\to$ acceptance rule $\to$ axis comparison $\to$ throughput.''
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Reused from prior work.} The entropy-based predictability test $D$ from \citet{shternshis2025predictability}, run at both adaptive $k$ and fixed $k = 2$; the all-offset construction from \citet{onofri2025emergence}, including its choice of $m = 5$ for Approximate Entropy; and the classical sub-tests from the NIST SP800-22 STS battery (Monobit, Runs, Approximate Entropy) and the TestU01 batteries (Alphabit, Rabbit). The strict gate (\cref{sec:acceptance-gates}) operationalises Onofri's all-offset framework with a fixed 0.80 pass-rate threshold.
\item \textbf{New in this thesis.} The overall methodological idea is to fuse the entropy-based $D$ test with the classical NIST and TestU01 sub-tests under a single all-offset acceptance rule, and to evaluate the resulting combined battery on the 24/7 continuous cryptocurrency market. On top of the reused components, the concrete new contributions are: the relaxed gate (\cref{sec:acceptance-gates}), the 1-second bar physical-time pipeline (\cref{sec:time-based-pipeline}), and the throughput metric (\cref{sec:throughput-metric}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Aggregation and Encoding}
\label{sec:aggregation-encoding}

Let the original tick sequence be $\{(t_i, p_i)\}_{i=0}^{N-1}$, where $t_i$ is the timestamp and $p_i$ is the trade price (0-based indexing, consistent with the code implementation). An aggregation level $\ell$ and a starting offset $o \in \{0, 1, \ldots, \ell - 1\}$ together define a sampled subsequence
\begin{equation}
q^{(\ell, o)}_j = p_{o + j\ell}, \qquad j = 0, 1, \ldots, \lfloor (N - o - 1) / \ell \rfloor.
\end{equation}
Its difference is $\Delta q^{(\ell, o)}_j = q^{(\ell, o)}_j - q^{(\ell, o)}_{j-1}$, for $j = 1, 2, \ldots, \lfloor (N - o - 1) / \ell \rfloor$. The bit encoding takes the sign:
\begin{equation}
b^{(\ell, o)}_j = \mathbbm{1}\!\left[\Delta q^{(\ell, o)}_j > 0\right].
\end{equation}

If $\Delta q^{(\ell, o)}_j = 0$, that bit is removed (\emph{drop-zero}, consistent with \citet{shternshis2025predictability}) and the later bits shift forward to keep the bit stream continuous. This removal is necessary: the share of zero differences is large at small $\ell$, and mapping zeros to either 0 or 1 by any fixed rule would inject a clear bias.

On the transaction-time axis, the unit of $\ell$ is ``number of trades''. On the physical-time axis, $p_i$ is first converted into ``per-second close price'' through the 1-second bar pipeline described in \cref{sec:time-based-pipeline}, and the unit of $\ell$ then becomes ``number of seconds''. All other encoding steps are the same on the two axes, which allows the two axes to be compared under the same encoding logic.

\begin{algorithm}
  \caption{Aggregation and bit encoding (single offset)}
  \label{alg:encode}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require Tick prices $\{p_0, p_1, \ldots, p_{N-1}\}$, level $\ell$, offset $o$
    \Ensure Bit stream $b$
    \State $q \gets [\,p_o,\; p_{o+\ell},\; p_{o+2\ell},\; \ldots\,]$
    \State $b \gets [\,]$
    \For{$j = 1$ \textbf{to} $|q| - 1$}
      \State $\Delta \gets q[j] - q[j-1]$
      \If{$\Delta > 0$}
        \State append $1$ to $b$
      \ElsIf{$\Delta < 0$}
        \State append $0$ to $b$
      \Else
        \State \textbf{skip} \Comment{drop-zero}
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $b$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{All-Offset Construction}
\label{sec:all-offset}

The all-offset construction described in this section comes directly from the ``$\ell$-samplings'' framework proposed by \citet{onofri2025emergence} - for each aggregation level $\ell$, $\ell$ separate bit streams are built, one from each starting offset ($o = 0, 1, \ldots, \ell - 1$). This thesis follows this construction, and only adjusts the parameters on the cryptocurrency data (per-asset $\ell$ grid, step, and upper bound).

For a fixed $\ell$, there are $\ell$ candidate offsets. The construction considers all $\ell$ bit streams together. Let $p^{(o)}$ denote the test $p$-value on each stream; the pass rate is defined as
\begin{equation}
r_{\text{pass}}(\ell, \alpha) = \frac{1}{N_{\text{valid}}(\ell)} \sum_{o \in \mathcal{O}_{\text{valid}}(\ell)} \mathbbm{1}\!\left[p^{(o)} \ge \alpha\right],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{O}_{\text{valid}}(\ell)$ is the set of offsets with bit count at least $\texttt{MIN\_BIT\_COUNT} = 2000$. This threshold is a conservative engineering floor that ensures enough samples to support the $\chi^2$ asymptotic approximation of the $D$ test. Because different offsets share the same underlying tick data, the $\ell$ bit streams are strongly correlated (especially for large $\ell$); their simultaneous passing provides robust evidence that ``the sequence passes from many starting points'', but it cannot be interpreted as the result of compounding independent tests. This point determines the design choice for the acceptance rule in \cref{sec:acceptance-gates}.

\section{Test Battery}
\label{sec:test-battery}

The test battery of this thesis is as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Predictability $\boldsymbol{D}$ (adaptive $k$).} The main statistic, with $k = \lfloor 0.5 \log_2 n \rfloor$.
\item \textbf{Predictability $\boldsymbol{D}$ (fixed $k = 2$).} A first-order diagnostic for pair-of-signs dependence between neighbouring symbols, covering both the bid-ask-bounce reversal and the persistent same-direction order-flow stylised facts.
\item \textbf{Monobit.} A basic frequency test that checks the overall 0/1 balance.
\item \textbf{Runs.} NIST-style, checking correlation between neighbouring bits. The NIST pre-frequency check is not implemented separately, since Monobit passing at $\alpha = 0.01$ implies $|\pi - 0.5| \le 1.288/\sqrt{n}$, which is strictly tighter than the NIST threshold $2/\sqrt{n}$; the check is therefore equivalently absorbed by the Monobit gate on every accepted~$\ell$.
\item \textbf{Approximate Entropy ($m = 5$).} The multi-window complexity difference, aligned with Table~4 of \citet{onofri2025emergence}. The NIST default is $m = 2$ (more stable on short sequences); $m = 5$ is more sensitive to short-range structure but requires $n \gtrsim 10 \cdot 2^m \approx 320$ for the asymptotic distribution to be reliable. This thesis uses $n \ge 2000$, well above this threshold.
\item \textbf{TestU01 Alphabit + Rabbit.} Cross-battery external validation for Experiments 3 and 4.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{When TestU01 is enabled.} Following the cross-battery design of \citet{onofri2025emergence}, the methodology of this thesis further includes the Alphabit and Rabbit sub-batteries of TestU01 as cross-battery external validation. Experiment 2 does not enable them - its goal is a coarse $\ell^*$ selection on the $\ell$ grid, and the per-(asset, $\ell$, offset) bit stream is only an intermediate output on the selection grid; its length is usually below the range where the cross-battery sanity check applies, so it is not a suitable input for cross-battery validation. Experiments 3 and 4 concatenate across months, assets, and offsets at the selected $\ell^*$, so that the length meets the sanity-check range in Table~3 of \citet{onofri2025emergence}, and the corresponding subsets of the Alphabit and Rabbit sub-tests are then enabled.

\textbf{Why both fixed $\boldsymbol{k = 2}$ and adaptive $\boldsymbol{k}$?} Adaptive $k = \lfloor 0.5 \log_2 n \rfloor$ is usually 5 to 6 on ultra-high-frequency monthly samples. The $D$ test then has $2^{k-1} \approx 16$ to $32$ contexts; first-order correlation -- whether the symbol reversal caused by the bid-ask bounce, or the same-direction persistence caused by sustained order flow - is diluted across more than ten or even tens of positions, and the statistical power drops quickly. \citet{shternshis2025predictability} provides empirical evidence consistent with this: on predictable days, with fixed $k = 2$, the ``too strong sign reversal'' pattern (i.e., $\hat{p}(00) + \hat{p}(11) < 0.5$) is significant; when $k$ is raised to about 5 to 6, this pattern is no longer significant. We therefore keep $D(k = 2)$ as a first-order diagnostic for pair-of-signs dependence, complementary to $D(\text{adaptive } k)$. The former captures short-range structure, while the latter measures the overall predictability. Experiment~1 (\cref{sec:exp1-results}) shows that on the cryptocurrency pairs studied here the first-order residual is positive on every asset - that is, the same-direction persistence side rather than the bid-ask reversal side. Its magnitude broadly moves with activity, but not in a strictly monotone order. This is exactly the kind of pair-wise structure $D(k = 2)$ is designed to detect.

\section{Acceptance Gates}
\label{sec:acceptance-gates}

The acceptance rule is the key step in the all-offset framework that maps the ``pass rate'' to ``whether $\ell$ is accepted''. This thesis uses two gates, introduced in chronological order.

\textbf{Strict gate.} \citet{onofri2025emergence} apply a qualitative acceptance criterion. We adopt a quantitative counterpart within the same framework: a fixed 0.80 pass-rate threshold, used consistently across all (asset, period) cells reported in this thesis. The strict gate requires the following three conditions to hold simultaneously:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_{\text{valid}}(\ell)            &\ge 0.80 \\
r_{\text{pass}}^{D}(\ell)         &\ge 0.80 \\
r_{\text{pass}}^{\text{Mono}}(\ell) &\ge 0.80
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha = 0.01$ is taken from \citet{onofri2025emergence}. The pass rates of Runs and $D(k = 2)$ are recorded as supplementary diagnostics only and do not enter the acceptance criterion; the reason is given in \cref{sec:test-battery}.

\textbf{Relaxed gate (a heuristic newly introduced in this thesis).} The all-offset bit streams are strongly correlated. From an engineering point of view, the final system only needs to pick one usable witness offset; but in the statistical selection process, a simple ``at-least-one passes'' rule would introduce a serious selection bias. If the underlying sequence still contains structural dependence, a large number of offsets may still produce one offset that passes the $\alpha$ threshold because of sample variation or low test power.

Furthermore, no symmetric formal correction is available in standard multiple-testing theory - Bonferroni / Šidák controls the probability that \emph{at least one} of $N$ tests falsely rejects $H_0$; tightening $\alpha$ makes each offset \emph{more likely} to pass, so the family-wise probability of being ``mistakenly accepted as random'' actually rises in the opposite direction. This thesis therefore neither adopts the ``at-least-one passes'' rule (selection bias) nor adopts the Bonferroni / Šidák formal correction (the direction is reversed), and instead uses the following heuristic redundancy criterion:
\begin{equation}
\text{accept}_{\text{relaxed}}(\ell) \iff n_{\text{pass}}(\ell) \ge \max\!\left(F,\; \lceil f \cdot N_{\text{valid}}(\ell) \rceil\right),
\end{equation}
where $n_{\text{pass}}(\ell)$ is the number of offsets that pass both $D$ and Monobit (at $\alpha = 0.01$, uncorrected); $(F, f)$ are two tuning parameters. This thesis takes $(F, f) = (3, 0.03)$: $F$ gives the minimum redundancy of joint confirmation across multiple offsets, and $f$ is about three times the $\alpha = 0.01$ noise floor. This rule is explicitly labelled in the thesis as a \emph{heuristic robustness check}, not a formal correction.

\begin{algorithm}
  \caption{All-offset acceptance check at level $\ell$}
  \label{alg:gate}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require Tick prices $\{p_i\}$, level $\ell$, $\text{gate} \in \{\text{strict}, \text{relaxed}\}$, $\alpha = 0.01$
    \Ensure $\text{accept}(\ell) \in \{\text{true}, \text{false}\}$; witness offset (if accepted)
    \State $\mathcal{O}_{\text{valid}} \gets \emptyset$
    \For{$o = 0$ \textbf{to} $\ell - 1$}
      \State $b \gets$ Algorithm~\ref{alg:encode} with $(p, \ell, o)$
      \If{$|b| \ge \texttt{MIN\_BIT\_COUNT}$ ($= 2000$)}
        \State compute and store $p^{D}(o),\; p^{\text{Mono}}(o)$ on $b$ \Comment{reused by both gates below}
        \State add $o$ to $\mathcal{O}_{\text{valid}}$
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \State $N_{\text{valid}} \gets |\mathcal{O}_{\text{valid}}|$
    \State $r_{\text{valid}} \gets N_{\text{valid}} / \ell$
    \If{$\text{gate} = \text{strict}$}
      \State $r_{\text{pass}}^{D} \gets |\{o \in \mathcal{O}_{\text{valid}} : p^{D}(o) \ge \alpha\}| / N_{\text{valid}}$
      \State $r_{\text{pass}}^{\text{Mono}} \gets |\{o \in \mathcal{O}_{\text{valid}} : p^{\text{Mono}}(o) \ge \alpha\}| / N_{\text{valid}}$
      \State \Return $r_{\text{valid}} \ge 0.80 \;\land\; r_{\text{pass}}^{D} \ge 0.80 \;\land\; r_{\text{pass}}^{\text{Mono}} \ge 0.80$
    \EndIf
    \If{$\text{gate} = \text{relaxed}$}
      \State $n_{\text{pass}} \gets |\{o \in \mathcal{O}_{\text{valid}} : p^{D}(o) \ge \alpha \;\land\; p^{\text{Mono}}(o) \ge \alpha\}|$
      \State \Return $n_{\text{pass}} \ge \max(F,\; \lceil f \cdot N_{\text{valid}} \rceil)$ \Comment{$(F, f) = (3, 0.03)$}
    \EndIf
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Selection rule for $\ell^*$.} The smallest $\ell$ for which Algorithm~\ref{alg:gate} returns true is recorded as the selected $\ell^*$. The specific $\ell$ grid (per-asset values, step, and bounds) is given in \cref{cha:experiments}.

\section{Time-Based Aggregation Pipeline}
\label{sec:time-based-pipeline}

\textbf{Adaptations for the cryptocurrency market.} To take advantage of the 24/7 continuous nature of the cryptocurrency market, this thesis introduces a physical-time aggregation axis based on 1-second bars, in addition to the transaction-time axis used above. On the transaction-time axis the throughput moves with the trading frequency, so the time needed to produce one bit varies with how active the market is. On the physical-time axis, $1/\ell^*$ gives a fixed sampling-rate and per-bit latency view, which is easier to describe as a Service Level Agreement (SLA), a formal commitment about bit-production latency. However, $1/\ell^*$ is not the final effective bit rate: because the drop-zero step removes seconds with no price change, the actual bit/s is usually slightly lower.

The 1-second bar pipeline (Algorithm~\ref{alg:1sbar}) groups trades into one-second buckets indexed by UTC time (Coordinated Universal Time, the global timestamp standard used by Binance). For each second that contains no trade, the bucket is filled with the price of the most recent prior trade (forward-fill). The standard difference + sign + drop-zero encoding from \cref{sec:aggregation-encoding} is then run on the resulting per-second close-price sequence. Forward-filled seconds have zero difference with their predecessor and are dropped at the drop-zero step, so they contribute no bit; the bit stream therefore contains only bits derived from real price changes between actually-traded seconds.

\begin{algorithm}
  \caption{1-second bar pipeline (physical-time axis)}
  \label{alg:1sbar}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require Trade stream $\{(t_i, p_i)\}$, level $\ell$ (seconds)
    \Ensure Bit streams $\{b^{(\ell, o)}\}$ for $o = 0, \ldots, \ell - 1$
    \Statex \Comment{Step 1: bucket by UTC second + forward-fill empty seconds}
    \Statex \Comment{(the first observed second always contains a trade, since data starts at the first trade)}
    \State group trades by $\lfloor t_i \rfloor$ \Comment{to whole UTC seconds}
    \For{each second $s$ in order}
      \If{$s$ has trade(s)}
        \State $c_s \gets$ price of last trade in $s$
      \Else
        \State $c_s \gets c_{s-1}$ \Comment{forward-fill}
      \EndIf
    \EndFor
    \Statex \Comment{Step 2 + 3: encode each offset via Algorithm~\ref{alg:encode}}
    \For{$o = 0$ \textbf{to} $\ell - 1$}
      \State $b^{(\ell, o)} \gets$ Algorithm~\ref{alg:encode} with $(\{c_s\}, \ell, o)$
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $\{b^{(\ell, o)}\}$
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The $\ell$ grid is taken as $[10, 600]$ with step 2. This covers both the optimistic case where ``second-level aggregation is enough'' and a longer (minute-level) aggregation for comparison. The upper bound of 600 seconds (=10 minutes) corresponds to a reasonable latency upper bound for password generation.

\section{Throughput Metric}
\label{sec:throughput-metric}

To align the methodological results with the downstream application, we record the throughput $r_b$ at each accepted $\ell^*$, defined as the effective output rate (in bits/s) of the witness offset bit stream at that $\ell^*$. On the transaction-time axis, $r_b$ is a realised rate driven by market activity, so it changes with the asset and the monthly trades/s. On the physical-time axis, $1/\ell^*$ gives the fixed sampling-rate and per-bit latency view used for SLA discussion. The effective $r_b$ is usually slightly lower than $1/\ell^*$, because drop-zero removes seconds that do not produce a bit.

\begin{itemize}

\textbf{aggTrades vs raw trades.} The aggTrades/s rate counts records in Binance's public aggregated stream, which merges raw fills that share timestamp, price, and direction into a single record. \textbf{Pre-processing.} On the transaction-time axis, we sort the monthly \texttt{aggTrades} by time and drop the rows where the price difference is zero. Dropping the zero-delta items is necessary on both axes, because mapping a zero to either bit (0 or 1) by any fixed rule would inject a clear bias (\cref{sec:aggregation-encoding}).

The five observations below are organised as a chain of evidence.

\textbf{Conditional-predictability tests and Runs reject every cell.} The entropy-predictability test $D$ (adaptive $k$ and fixed $k = 2$) tests whether the next bit depends on previous bits; Approximate Entropy measures the complexity of short patterns; the Runs test examines the run structure of adjacent bits. All four tests reject all 75 cells at $\alpha = 0.01$ --- 15 out of 15 months for every asset (\cref{tab:exp1-summary}), with the Runs $p$-value underflowing to $0$ on 73 of them. Unlike Monobit, these tests do not rely only on the marginal frequency moving away from $0.5$: even for SOL, where $p(1)$ stays close to $0.5$ and Monobit rejects only 4 of 15 months, $D$, ApEn, and Runs reject all 15 months. This is the most direct evidence that the raw tick stream is not random, and the evidence that depends least on any marginal assumption.

\textbf{Monobit alone misses non-randomness.} Of the 75 cells, $22$ are not rejected by Monobit at $\alpha = 0.01$: $11$ on SOL, $7$ on DOGE, $2$ each on BNB and ETH, and $0$ on BTC. As the previous observation shows, these cells do not pass the randomness checks at the conditional-predictability or run-structure level. The non-rejections concentrate on the low-bps assets, because their $p(1)$ is closer to $0.5$ and their shorter monthly bit streams give Monobit less power to detect a small marginal deviation. The apparent ``Monobit reject count rises with bps'' trend should therefore not be read as evidence that the low-bps assets are more random; it shows instead that a marginal-frequency test on its own is not sufficient to assess randomness.

\textbf{Lag-1 autocorrelation is a diagnostic signal of first-order dependence.} The five per-asset medians are all positive: ETH $+0.745$, BTC $+0.636$, DOGE $+0.477$, BNB $+0.366$, SOL $+0.095$ (\cref{tab:exp1-summary}). Their magnitude broadly co-varies with bps but is not strictly monotone; on a per-month basis (\cref{fig:exp1-distributions}, top-right) the distribution stays well above zero on BTC, ETH, DOGE, and BNB, and is close to zero on SOL. The role of this result is not to claim that activity determines autocorrelation, but to give an intuitive diagnostic of the non-independence of the raw tick stream: even when the marginal frequency is close to $0.5$, adjacent bits are still positively correlated. The pattern is consistent with persistent same-side order flow --- when a large taker order walks the book it can produce several same-direction trades in a row, and the long memory of trade-sign autocorrelation~\citep{lillo2004longmemory} adds further positive correlation. Lag-1 autocorrelation here therefore serves mainly as a diagnostic indicator of short-range dependence in the raw tick stream.

\textbf{The longest run is a structural cryptographic risk signal.} The $L_{\max}$ column of \cref{tab:exp1-summary} reports the largest run length observed across the 15 monthly cells per asset: BTC $12{,}039$, ETH $15{,}899$, DOGE $5{,}071$, BNB $2{,}069$, SOL $792$. The i.i.d. random expectation is $E[L_{\max}] \approx \log_2 n$, which for our monthly bit-stream lengths $n \sim 10^6$ to $4 \times 10^7$ is about $20$ to $25$. Every observed $L_{\max}$ is two to three orders of magnitude above this baseline. As an extreme statistic over 15 months, $L_{\max}$ fluctuates considerably and its ordering is not strictly the same as the bps ordering (ETH above BTC, DOGE above BNB); but ``far above the i.i.d. expectation'' holds without exception for all five assets. The longest run is therefore not used as a standalone acceptance criterion, but as an intuitive explanation of how the raw tick stream fails as a cryptographic random source: if the sampling window for a seed or password falls inside such a long run, the output degenerates into a long block of all $0$s or all $1$s.

\textbf{The bit emission rate sets an upper bound on downstream throughput.} Per-cell bit rates range from $0.68$ bps (DOGE, 2026-03) to $15.52$ bps (BTC, 2026-02); the per-asset medians are BTC $10.68$, ETH $9.56$, BNB $2.49$, DOGE $2.02$, SOL $1.74$ bps (\cref{tab:exp1-summary}). The top two match the raw trades/s ranking in \cref{tab:trades-per-second} (BTC then ETH), but the bottom three are reversed: although SOL has a higher raw trades/s than BNB and DOGE, its zero-delta ratio is the largest of the five (median $\approx 0.58$ across the 15 months), so after dropping the zero-difference events SOL is left with fewer bit events than BNB (zero-delta ratio $\approx 0.44$) and ends up last in the bps ranking. The baseline throughput therefore depends not only on trades/s but also on the per-asset zero-delta ratio, and it forms the upper bound for any post-aggregation throughput estimate.

\subsection{Implications}
\label{sec:exp1-implications}

Experiment~1 gives a negative but quantitative conclusion: the sign-encoded raw tick sequence cannot be used as an RNG. Although the marginal frequency is nearly balanced, Runs and the conditional-predictability battery reject all $75$ (asset, month) cells; the lag-1 autocorrelation and the longest run further show that the failure comes from adjacent dependence and long structure, not merely from a shift in the 0/1 ratio. Monobit's misses on the lower-activity assets ($22$ of the $75$ cells fail to reject) show that later experiments cannot rely on a marginal-frequency test alone. This conclusion provides three direct anchors for Experiment~2.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Aggregation is necessary.} The $\ell = 1$ raw tick stream is rejected on every cell by Runs, ApEn, and $D$, so Experiment~2 must reduce the conditional dependence through aggregation rather than search for an acceptable window in the raw sign stream.
\item \textbf{First-order residual structure as a diagnostic comparator.} The lag-1 autocorrelation is positive on all five assets, which shows a stable first-order dependence in the raw tick sign stream; its magnitude broadly co-varies with bps, which suggests the diagnostic needs to cover a range of activity levels. Experiment~2 therefore uses it as a comparator between the transaction-time and one-second-bar aggregation axes. The later experiments do not assume this structure will be fully removed; instead they test how far each aggregation axis can reduce this short-range dependence.
\item \textbf{Upper bound on throughput.} The baseline bits/s scale across the 75 monthly cells (1--16 bps) is the ceiling for the throughput metric in \cref{sec:throughput-metric} and for the entropy-rate discussion in Experiment~3; any later aggregation trades throughput below this bound for stronger randomness diagnostics.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experiment 2: Aggregation}
\label{sec:exp2}

Experiment~2 answers the positive side of RQ1. Experiment~1 has shown that the raw tick stream is not usable, so aggregation is necessary; here we test how deep the aggregation must be on each of the two time axes to make the bit stream pass standard randomness tests at $\alpha = 0.01$.

This section proceeds in four steps. Each step exposes a problem that the next step then addresses.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Transaction-time single offset} (\cref{sec:exp2-single-offset}): we fix the starting offset $o = 0$ as a quick locating step, get a first estimate of $\ell^*$, and show why a single offset is not a robust criterion.
\item \textbf{Transaction-time all-offset strict gate} (\cref{sec:exp2-strict-gate}): we move to the full all-offset construction and use the strict $80\%$ pass-rate gate to test robustness across offsets.
\item \textbf{Transaction-time all-offset relaxed gate} (\cref{sec:exp2-relaxed-gate}): with a small offset redundancy preserved, we relax the gate to deal with the coverage and throughput cost of the strict gate.
\item \textbf{Physical-time 1-second bars} (\cref{sec:exp2-1sbars}): we switch to the physical-time axis and check whether the short-range dependence behaves differently under a different sampling axis, and whether a fixed-time SLA is more deployment-friendly.
\end{enumerate}

\cref{sec:exp2-implications} then collects the implications from the four steps and gives the configuration that downstream experiments will use.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp2-setup}

The shared setup below covers \cref{sec:exp2-single-offset,sec:exp2-strict-gate,sec:exp2-relaxed-gate,sec:exp2-1sbars}. Each subsection only states the differences in the $\ell$ grid.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Asset universe.} The same five USDT spot pairs as Experiment~1 (\texttt{BTCUSDT}, \texttt{ETHUSDT}, \texttt{BNBUSDT}, \texttt{SOLUSDT}, \texttt{DOGEUSDT}; see \cref{sec:overview}).
\item \textbf{Window.} The same 15-month sample (2025-01 to 2026-03). The diagnostic granularity is monthly: one bit stream per (asset, month) cell, $5 \times 15 = 75$ cells in total.
\item \textbf{Encoding.} \cref{alg:encode}: on the transaction-time axis $\ell$ has the unit ``number of trades''; on the physical-time axis the pipeline in \cref{sec:time-based-pipeline} first turns the price series into one closing price per second, and $\ell$ becomes ``number of seconds''. The drop-zero step is the same on both axes.
\item \textbf{All-offset construction.} \cref{sec:all-offset}: for each $\ell$ we build $\ell$ offset bit streams and test them independently. \cref{sec:exp2-single-offset} first uses the simplified single-offset version ($o = 0$ only, one stream); from \cref{sec:exp2-strict-gate} on we use the full all-offset version.
\item \textbf{Acceptance gates.} The main acceptance rule is $D$ (adaptive $k$) + Monobit: the single-offset version asks for both $p$-values to be $\geq \alpha$; the all-offset strict gate asks for both pass rates to be $\geq 0.80$; the relaxed gate is the heuristic version in \cref{sec:acceptance-gates}. Runs, $D(k = 2)$, ApEn, and the Shannon bias are still recorded as auxiliary diagnostics. Runs is also added explicitly in \cref{sec:exp2-1sbars} as the ``+Runs'' gate.
\item \textbf{$\ell$ grid.} \cref{sec:exp2-single-offset} and \cref{sec:exp2-strict-gate} use $\ell$ from $50$ to $2000$ with step $25$; \cref{sec:exp2-relaxed-gate} uses $10$ to $2000$ with step $2$; \cref{sec:exp2-1sbars} uses $10$ to $600$ with step $1$.
\item \textbf{Reproduction.} The results below can be reproduced by running the four Experiment~2 runner scripts under \texttt{scripts/}: single-offset, all-offset strict, all-offset relaxed, and 1-second bars.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Selected $\ell^*$ shows a coarse activity-based layering.} The per-asset medians (BTC $\approx 1300$, ETH $\approx 725$, BNB $\approx 275$, SOL $\approx 225$, DOGE $\approx 225$) show that BTC and ETH need a clearly deeper aggregation in the $[50, 2000]$ range, while the three lower-activity assets reach the threshold around $200$--$300$. This is in the same direction as the ``higher trades/s, larger required $\ell$'' relation reported by \citet{onofri2025emergence} on 8 US stocks plus 1 ETF; but our result should be read only as a coarse layering, not as a strict monotone ranking between all five assets.

\textbf{Single offset only locates the magnitude; it is not the final acceptance scheme.} In 2025-01 and 2025-08, BTC does not let $D$ and Monobit pass together for any $\ell$ in $[50, 2000]$ (see \cref{tab:appendix-a1}). This shows that fixing $o = 0$ can give a first estimate of $\ell^*$ but cannot guarantee full coverage; these failures also cannot be explained only by single-month trades/s. Single offset is therefore used only as a locating step.

\textbf{The real problem is offset robustness.} Even if some $\ell$ passes at $o = 0$, we cannot say whether the $\ell - 1$ other offset streams will also pass. If the pass result depends strongly on $o$, the ``acceptable'' label of that cell is only the result at one starting point. \cref{sec:exp2-strict-gate} therefore upgrades the fixed-offset diagnostic to ``at least $80\%$ of the offsets pass together'', which also responds to the BTC empty-cell problem and the robustness problem.

So ``passing the strict gate'' does not mean ``the first-order structure is removed''; it only guarantees that $D$ at adaptive $k$ and Monobit are both satisfied. This residual is a stable feature on the transaction-time axis, and is one of the reasons we later compare the physical-time axis in \cref{sec:exp2-1sbars}.

\textbf{Two engineering problems of the strict gate motivate the relaxed gate in \cref{sec:exp2-relaxed-gate}.} First, BTC has no acceptable $\ell \leq 2000$ on 8 months, which shows that the $80\%$ requirement is too strict for the highest-activity asset. Second, the month-to-month range of selected $\ell^*$ is wide, and the coarse grid further limits the estimation precision. \cref{sec:exp2-relaxed-gate} therefore turns to the heuristic relaxed gate and also refines the $\ell$ grid to step $2$.

\textbf{Bonferroni / \v{S}id\'ak comparison gives smaller $\ell^*$.} As a directional comparison, a Bonferroni / \v{S}id\'ak version on 2025~Q1 gives systematically smaller selected $\ell^*$ than the heuristic relaxed gate. This subsection closes the chain on the transaction-time axis: single offset $\to$ strict gate $\to$ relaxed gate. The last two trade off robustness, coverage and throughput. The relaxed gate gives an engineering candidate with full $75/75$ acceptance, but it is still a heuristic and does not give a stronger statistical guarantee. Two deployment-level questions still remain: first, $D(k = 2)$ and Runs are almost always rejected near the selected $\ell^*$, so whether the short-range dependence is amplified by the sampling axis needs to be tested; second, transaction-time throughput varies with intra-month trades/s, so it is hard to write a fixed per-bit latency. \cref{sec:exp2-1sbars} therefore switches to the physical-time axis (1-second bar) to check both the independence residual and the fixed-time SLA.

\textbf{The 1-second bar base gate covers $70/75$ cells on the physical-time axis.} The base gate gives 70 acceptable cells out of 75, which shows that the axis change still finds a second-level $\ell^*$ on most cells. \textbf{Adding Runs to the gate moves the coverage from $70/75$ to $62/75$, which is the key independence result on the 1-second bar axis.} To compare the two axes under the same criterion, we also add Runs to the transaction-time strict gate and to the 1-second bar gate. Transaction-time strict+Runs covers only $48/75$ cells (\cref{tab:appendix-a3}), while 1-second bar +Runs covers $62/75$; adding ApEn after that keeps the same $62/75$. This comparison shows that the first-order residual which is hard to handle on the transaction-time axis is clearly reduced on the physical-time axis.

\textbf{This independence improvement is connected to the aggregation axis.} The same aggTrades data still keeps a strong first-order residual on the transaction-time axis, but on the physical-time axis most cells get a clear reduction. This result does not support the reading that ``the first-order residual comes entirely from the source data and cannot be reduced''; a more reasonable reading is that sampling by equal trade counts concentrates same-direction order flow into adjacent bits, while a 1-second bar spreads out this local concentration. This is consistent with the Experiment~1 diagnostic that lag-1 autocorrelation is positive on all five assets, with a magnitude broadly co-varying with activity.

\textbf{The SLA-friendliness on the physical-time axis comes from the fixed sampling rate.} Transaction-time throughput varies with intra-month trades/s; on the physical-time axis, $1 / \ell^*$ gives a fixed sampling rate and a fixed per-bit latency SLA. The drop-zero step still filters out zero-difference seconds, so the actual bit per second is a little below $1/\ell^*$, but the magnitude is similar. The descriptive rate corresponding to the $\ell^*$ medians in \cref{tab:exp2-1sbars-summary} falls roughly in the $0.01$--$0.03$ bps range. Adding Runs reduces coverage, and on some assets and months it also raises the latency.

\FloatBarrier % keep Figure 4.5 + Table 4.6 inside §4.3.5

\subsection{Implications}
\label{sec:exp2-implications}

The main role of Experiment~2 is to choose the aggregation axis for the downstream pipeline; both axes can produce acceptable bit streams, but they serve different engineering purposes. This subsection summarises the four conclusions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Physical-time should be used as the main aggregation axis.} The bit rate on transaction-time varies with trades/s, so it is more suitable as a statistical construction and as a supplementary comparison, not as the main setup for the downstream pipeline. In contrast, the $\ell$ on physical-time is in seconds, and the selected $\ell^*$ can be read directly as a wall-clock sampling schedule and a per-bit latency value.

\item \textbf{Physical-time is more suitable as the input configuration for the extended tests.} Under the +Runs criterion, the transaction-time strict gate drops from $63/75$ to $48/75$ accepted cells (a within-axis loss of $24\%$), while the 1-second bar base gate drops from $70/75$ to $62/75$ accepted cells (a within-axis loss of $11\%$); adding ApEn after that keeps the same $62/75$. The within-axis coverage loss is clearly larger on transaction-time, which shows that the Runs / $D(k=2)$ residual on transaction-time is at least partly amplified by the sampling axis. The later entropy-rate analysis, the additional NIST sub-tests, and the TestU01 sanity check therefore use the bit streams selected on the physical-time axis.

\item \textbf{Transaction-time relaxed gate is kept as a supplementary configuration.} It shows that the trade-count axis can also reach full coverage by adding offset redundancy through a heuristic, and it can serve as a potential fallback for the cells where the 1-second bar base gate fails. But it relies on a heuristic gate, and its throughput varies with market activity, so it is not used as the main configuration. Whether it is actually used as a fallback in Experiment~3 is decided in \cref{sec:exp3}.

\item \textbf{Throughput is reported only as a magnitude anchor.} The per-cell bit rate of the selected cells in Experiment~2 is at the $10^{-2}$ bps order, which means the aggregation buys a stronger randomness diagnostic at the cost of a clearly lower bit emission rate. The specific bits/day, the length of bit streams after concatenation, the applicability of the extended test battery, and the end-to-end latency are left to the later experiments.
\end{enumerate}
